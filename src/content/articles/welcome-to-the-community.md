_Adapted from my introduction video, published on 9 September 2026. This is my personal application story, edited from the transcript for clarity. It describes where I was in the process when I recorded the video._

I was born in Perth, Australia, but my grandfather’s family came from Korčula, an island in Croatia. After years of researching records, gathering documents and wondering whether it would ever happen, I received the news I had been waiting for: my application for Croatian citizenship by descent had been approved.

By the time I recorded this introduction, I was in Croatia. Finding out about the approval had been enough to get me on a flight. But getting to that point had been a much longer journey.

## Why I started looking into my Croatian roots

I began exploring citizenship while I was living in Australia. During the pandemic, I was feeling stuck and isolated, and I wanted to learn more about my family’s history.

That curiosity led to a practical question: could my connection to Croatia also give me a path to citizenship?

At first, I wasn’t confident that I would be able to do it. There were problems in the family records, documents to find in different countries and a process I had to work out as I went along.

## My grandfather had two names—and the wrong birth year on a certificate

One of the biggest obstacles was proving the connection to my grandfather.

His original name was Jakov Anton Tomić, but in Australian records he was known as James Tomich. As I explain in the video, this wasn’t a straightforward legal name change with a neat document connecting the two names.

There was another discrepancy: his death certificate recorded his birth year as 1924, while he was actually born in 1923.

Those details became real obstacles. I knew who my grandfather was, but I needed the paperwork to establish the family connection despite the differences in the records. For a while, the incorrect birth year was one of the reasons I doubted whether I could get citizenship at all.

Once I was able to prove the family line, the application began to feel possible.

## Gathering documents across several countries

My family history meant the paperwork wasn’t all in one place. My grandfather was born in Croatia, my father in New Zealand, my mother in England and I was born in Australia.

I had to obtain documents from different places and work through their different procedures. That involved more than ordering certificates: I also had to deal with the authentication and translation steps for my application.

I contacted consular and embassy offices to work out who I needed to speak to. I also tried contacting companies for help, but at the time I struggled to get replies. In the end, I did much of the work myself.

It was a lesson in how a family story can cross borders much more easily than the paperwork needed to document it.

## One missing form cost me another eight months

One experience from the process still stands out.

I arrived for an appointment and discovered that I was missing a form. I thought I could leave, get it sorted in a couple of hours and return. Instead, I was told I needed another appointment.

The next appointment available to me was eight months away.

That was a substantial delay over something that had seemed small. It is why, when I talk about my experience, I emphasise checking the requirements carefully before attending an appointment.

The eight-month wait was what happened in my case. It isn’t a general estimate of appointment availability or application processing times.

## The second time, I brought a much more thorough application

When I prepared for the next appointment, I wanted to avoid another missing piece of paperwork.

I collected immigration records and went further back into the family history. I even obtained my great-grandfather’s birth and death certificates, although my application was based on the connection through my grandfather.

By the time I handed everything over, I had a large stack of documents. The person receiving them commented on how thorough the application was.

Even then, I wasn’t certain that I would be approved. I knew there would still be a wait. Gathering extra records was a choice I made for my circumstances, not a document checklist that everyone should copy.

## Finally getting the decision—and coming to Croatia

The process took years. When I finally learned that my citizenship had been approved, I travelled to Croatia soon afterwards.

In the introduction video, I was speaking from Croatia about two weeks after hearing the news. There was still more administration ahead. At that point, I was waiting for a newly issued birth certificate and preparing for further authentication and translation steps.

I hadn’t reached the end of every document-related task. But I had received the citizenship decision I had spent so long working towards.

## What my experience can—and cannot—tell you

My story shows some of the practical difficulties I encountered: inconsistent records, paperwork across countries, an incomplete appointment and a long wait.

It does not establish whether someone else qualifies. The video also discusses ancestry, language requirements and possible timelines. Those comments should not be treated as a complete statement of the current rules or a promise about how quickly another case will move.

Confirm the route, documents and current instructions for your own circumstances with the competent Croatian authority or a qualified adviser.

## Why I’m sharing the journey

Getting approved is also the beginning of what I want to do with Croatian By Descent Community.

I want to meet people with Croatian ancestry, people who have returned and people who have built a new life here. I want to hear what brought them to Croatia, what the experience has been like and what they would tell someone considering the same step.

Some people want a stronger connection to their roots. Others are looking for a change in life or a different place to call home. I want this community to make room for those different stories.

## Reader’s companion: researching your own family story

_The following sections are an editorial companion to Carl’s account. They offer ways to organise your research; they are not additional events from his life or statements taken from the video._

Carl’s introduction contains several different stories at once. There is a family-history investigation, an application process, a delayed appointment, an eventual decision and the beginning of a new chapter in Croatia. Keeping those strands separate makes the account more useful. It helps a reader distinguish the things that can be prepared in advance from the things that depended on Carl’s particular circumstances.

Someone beginning a similar investigation might recognise the emotional starting point immediately: a familiar surname, a grandparent’s birthplace or the sense that part of the family story has remained unexplored. That connection is meaningful even before an application is considered. Citizenship research, however, asks an additional question: what can be established through records and assessed under the applicable rules? A family memory and an official document serve different purposes, and both deserve careful handling.

The practical exercises below start with the difficulties described in the video. They do not suggest that collecting Carl’s documents will reproduce his result. Their purpose is to help you arrive at your own conversations with officials, archives and advisers with clearer questions and better organised information.

### Make a family timeline before making a shopping list

Draw a line from yourself back to the relative whose connection you want to investigate. For each person, record a full name, any other names used, dates and places of birth, relationships, marriages and moves between countries. Leave gaps visible. A question mark is more useful than a confident guess that later becomes embedded in every request you send.

Create a separate column for the source of each entry. A birth certificate, a passenger record, an inscription on a photograph and a cousin’s recollection are different kinds of evidence. Recording the source prevents a remembered detail from silently becoming a supposedly verified fact. It also lets someone helping you see which questions can be answered from documents you already hold.

Carl’s family documents spanned Croatia, New Zealand, England and Australia. That detail illustrates why a single-country search may be insufficient, without establishing that every applicant needs documents from four countries. Start with the actual people and events in your own family line. The necessary records should follow the facts of that line and the authority’s instructions, rather than an impressive-looking list copied from another person’s application.

### Treat different names as a research question

The contrast between Jakov Anton Tomić and James Tomich is a central part of Carl’s story. A reader researching a different family may encounter changes in spelling, omitted accents, anglicised names or variations in the order of given names. Those observations are starting points for investigation. They are not, by themselves, proof that two records belong to the same person.

Keep the spelling from each original record intact in your notes. Alongside it, identify the matching and conflicting details: relatives, dates, addresses, occupations or places. A simple comparison table can make the issue easier to explain to an archive or qualified adviser. It is especially useful when a name looks similar but another important detail does not match.

Do not edit a scan to make a record agree with the rest of the file. Ask the relevant issuing authority about its correction procedure where appropriate, and ask the authority assessing your application how a discrepancy should be documented. An informal explanation, a corrected certificate and an officially accepted supporting record are different things. Which one is relevant depends on the problem you actually have.

### Keep a discrepancy log, including the small details

The birth-year discrepancy in Carl’s account was only one year, but it mattered to his research. That is a useful reminder to examine small differences before an appointment. Compare dates, parents’ names and places across the records that link each generation. Note which original document contains each version and whether you have checked the image rather than relying on a searchable transcription.

Give every discrepancy a status: not yet investigated, query sent, response received or resolved for the purpose of the application. Add the date and the name of the organisation you contacted. This is an organisational method, not a legal standard, but it stops the same uncertainty being rediscovered repeatedly as a file becomes larger.

Where a response does not answer the question, preserve it and write a narrower follow-up. For example, an archive may confirm that a record exists without explaining whether the copy it provides can serve your intended purpose. Separating those questions can save another round of correspondence. Avoid assuming that an unanswered issue has become unimportant simply because the rest of the paperwork is ready.

### Plan the order of document requests

For every potential record, write down the issuing office, application method, expected delivery arrangements and the instructions you still need to confirm. Keep costs and estimated completion dates separate from confirmed requirements. A quotation from a service provider tells you what that provider offers; it does not necessarily establish what the Croatian authority will accept.

Authentication and translation can introduce dependencies. Before commissioning either, ask what form of record is needed and whether a particular sequence is required in your circumstances. A translation ordered too early may need to be repeated if the underlying document is later replaced or corrected. The aim is to understand the sequence before paying for avoidable work.

Maintain a secure working copy of your inventory, but remember that family certificates contain personal information about other people too. Send records only to the offices or professionals who need them. If you ask a public community for help interpreting a document, remove document numbers, addresses, signatures and unrelated family information from the version you share.

### Prepare for the appointment as a separate stage

Carl’s missed form led to an eight-month wait for another appointment. His experience cannot predict another office’s availability, but it explains why appointment preparation deserves its own check. A file can contain extensive family research and still be missing a current application form, a required signature or another item specified for the appointment.

Use the instructions from the office that will receive your application. Confirm that they apply to your route and circumstances, and save the date on which you checked them. If something is ambiguous, identify that specific point in your enquiry. A short question about one unclear requirement is easier to answer accurately than a request for someone to approve an entire unstructured folder.

Before attending, compare the appointment instructions with the physical documents you intend to take. Check whether originals and copies are distinguished, whether any document has been replaced since translation, and whether names and contact details are consistent with the records. Keep the material ordered so you can find an item when asked. This exercise improves organisation; it does not guarantee that an application will be accepted as complete.

### Use personal timelines carefully

There are at least three clocks in a story like this: the time spent researching before submission, the wait for an appointment and the period after an application has been submitted. A fourth can begin after a decision, when a person works through registration or identity-document steps. Combining all of them into a single number makes comparisons misleading.

When reading another applicant’s account, ask which stage the person is describing and when it happened. An appointment delay is not the same thing as an assessment period. An estimate from a friend is not a commitment from an authority. Even a carefully documented completed case cannot establish the outcome or duration of the next one.

For your own planning, write down the decisions that genuinely depend on a confirmed result. A flight, a lease, employment arrangements and school plans can have very different consequences if dates shift. Carl chose to travel soon after hearing his news. His personal choice is part of the story, rather than a sequence that another household should automatically follow.

### Understand where the introduction ends

The video captures a particular moment: Carl had learned of his approval, travelled to Croatia and still had administrative tasks ahead. It does not document every later stage. Reading the article with that boundary in mind prevents an introduction from being mistaken for a completed record of passport issuance, relocation or long-term settlement.

The same boundary is useful when sharing your own experience. State the date, the stage reached and the questions still open. If you later publish an update, distinguish new information from what you knew at the time. That makes a personal account more trustworthy and more useful to people who discover it months or years later.

The most valuable contribution to a community is often a precise, limited observation: which record caused a problem, how a question was clarified, or what an appointment required in a specific case. It does not need to become a universal rule. Carl’s story is compelling because the obstacles and the eventual approval are concrete. Keeping that precision is more helpful than turning his experience into a promise.

### Continue with questions that match your own circumstances

If the video has prompted you to investigate your roots, begin with one manageable task: identify the family connection you want to document and write down the strongest record you already have for it. Then list the most important missing link. That gives your next enquiry a clear purpose, whether it goes to a relative, an archive or an official office.

For questions about eligibility and application requirements, use the official sources below. For the human side of the journey, keep following the community’s personal stories and interviews. Those two kinds of information work well together when their roles remain clear: official guidance helps establish the applicable process, while a person’s account shows what navigating that process felt like in one real life.
