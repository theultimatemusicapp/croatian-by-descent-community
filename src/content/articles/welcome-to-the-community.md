_Adapted from my introduction video, published on 9 September 2026. This is my personal application story, edited from the transcript for clarity. It describes where I was in the process when I recorded the video._

I was born in Perth, Australia, but my grandfather’s family came from Korčula, an island in Croatia. After years of researching records, gathering documents and wondering whether it would ever happen, I received the news I had been waiting for: my application for Croatian citizenship by descent had been approved.

By the time I recorded this introduction, I was in Croatia. Finding out about the approval had been enough to get me on a flight. But getting to that point had been a much longer journey.

## Why I started looking into my Croatian roots

I began exploring citizenship while I was living in Australia. During the pandemic, I was feeling stuck and isolated, and I wanted to learn more about my family’s history.

That curiosity led to a practical question: could my connection to Croatia also give me a path to citizenship?

At first, I wasn’t confident that I would be able to do it. There were problems in the family records, documents to find in different countries and a process I had to work out as I went along.

## My grandfather had two names—and the wrong birth year on a certificate

One of the biggest obstacles was proving the connection to my grandfather.

His original name was Jakov Anton Tomić, but in Australian records he was known as James Tomich. As I explain in the video, this wasn’t a straightforward legal name change with a neat document connecting the two names.

There was another discrepancy: his death certificate recorded his birth year as 1924, while he was actually born in 1923.

Those details became real obstacles. I knew who my grandfather was, but I needed the paperwork to establish the family connection despite the differences in the records. For a while, the incorrect birth year was one of the reasons I doubted whether I could get citizenship at all.

Once I was able to prove the family line, the application began to feel possible.

## Gathering documents across several countries

My family history meant the paperwork wasn’t all in one place. My grandfather was born in Croatia, my father in New Zealand, my mother in England and I was born in Australia.

I had to obtain documents from different places and work through their different procedures. That involved more than ordering certificates: I also had to deal with the authentication and translation steps for my application.

I contacted consular and embassy offices to work out who I needed to speak to. I also tried contacting companies for help, but at the time I struggled to get replies. In the end, I did much of the work myself.

It was a lesson in how a family story can cross borders much more easily than the paperwork needed to document it.

## One missing form cost me another eight months

One experience from the process still stands out.

I arrived for an appointment and discovered that I was missing a form. I thought I could leave, get it sorted in a couple of hours and return. Instead, I was told I needed another appointment.

The next appointment available to me was eight months away.

That was a substantial delay over something that had seemed small. It is why, when I talk about my experience, I emphasise checking the requirements carefully before attending an appointment.

The eight-month wait was what happened in my case. It isn’t a general estimate of appointment availability or application processing times.

## The second time, I brought a much more thorough application

When I prepared for the next appointment, I wanted to avoid another missing piece of paperwork.

I collected immigration records and went further back into the family history. I even obtained my great-grandfather’s birth and death certificates, although my application was based on the connection through my grandfather.

By the time I handed everything over, I had a large stack of documents. The person receiving them commented on how thorough the application was.

Even then, I wasn’t certain that I would be approved. I knew there would still be a wait. Gathering extra records was a choice I made for my circumstances, not a document checklist that everyone should copy.

## Finally getting the decision—and coming to Croatia

The process took years. When I finally learned that my citizenship had been approved, I travelled to Croatia soon afterwards.

In the introduction video, I was speaking from Croatia about two weeks after hearing the news. There was still more administration ahead. At that point, I was waiting for a newly issued birth certificate and preparing for further authentication and translation steps.

I hadn’t reached the end of every document-related task. But I had received the citizenship decision I had spent so long working towards.

## What my experience can—and cannot—tell you

My story shows some of the practical difficulties I encountered: inconsistent records, paperwork across countries, an incomplete appointment and a long wait.

It does not establish whether someone else qualifies. The video also discusses ancestry, language requirements and possible timelines. Those comments should not be treated as a complete statement of the current rules or a promise about how quickly another case will move.

**Editorial context:** Croatia’s Ministry of the Interior sets out different grounds for acquiring citizenship and the supporting evidence relevant to them. Its guidance also explains that legalisation requirements for foreign documents can be affected by international agreements. Confirm the route, documents and current instructions for your own circumstances with the competent Croatian authority or a qualified adviser.

## Why I’m sharing the journey

Getting approved is also the beginning of what I want to do with Croatian By Descent Community.

I want to meet people with Croatian ancestry, people who have returned and people who have built a new life here. I want to hear what brought them to Croatia, what the experience has been like and what they would tell someone considering the same step.

Some people want a stronger connection to their roots. Others are looking for a change in life or a different place to call home. I want this community to make room for those different stories.
