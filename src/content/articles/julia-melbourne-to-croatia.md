_Adapted from Julia’s interview with Carl Tomich, using the published video description and transcript. Her memories, assessments and plans are presented as her own account. This article includes a brief, non-graphic discussion of domestic abuse and bereavement._

Julia was born in Zagreb and moved to Melbourne when she was a year old. Today she lives in Samobor, near Zagreb, but the journey between those two points has been anything but straightforward. There have been teenage discoveries, family reunions, long absences, cancelled flights and plans that changed after she thought she knew what her new life would look like.

In her conversation with Croatian By Descent Community, she describes Croatia as a place where she has found peace. She also talks openly about missing Australia, finding unfamiliar systems difficult and learning that an emotional connection to a country does not make every practical part of living there easy. Both sides belong in the story.

Her advice to people considering a return is to separate the appeal of family memories from the realities of an ordinary week. The phrase she uses is direct: “remove the nostalgia first.” It is not a rejection of Croatia. It is the perspective of someone whose attachment has survived the experience of trying to build a life there.

## A childhood in Melbourne, with a family history elsewhere

Julia places her family’s departure from Yugoslavia at around 1971. In her account, her parents left under difficult political circumstances, with help from a priest and a network that supported their arrival in Australia. She was too young to experience that move as an adult decision. Melbourne became the place where she grew up.

She recalls that neither parent initially spoke English, yet both eventually found work. Looking back, she wonders how they managed the unfamiliar systems and the language barrier. The detail becomes significant later in the interview, when she describes facing her own version of those difficulties in Croatia. Her parents’ migration and her return happened in different circumstances, but both involved learning how to function somewhere new.

Growing up in Australia did not mean that she already understood the family connections waiting in Croatia. Her teenage visit would reveal relatives she had barely known existed. The interview challenges an easy assumption about diaspora identity: knowing that a family came from somewhere is not necessarily the same as knowing the people, language and relationships that make that place tangible.

For Julia, that knowledge developed through direct encounters. Croatia was not simply an inherited label that remained unchanged from childhood. Her relationship with it was formed over several visits, and each return added something different to what the country meant to her.

## Returning at sixteen and meeting an unfamiliar family

In 1986, when Julia was sixteen, her mother decided to return with the children. Her father stayed in Australia to continue working. Julia remembers arriving without knowing Croatian and with little understanding of why a place she did not know was being presented as home.

The year that followed introduced her to cousins, an aunt and an uncle. Those relationships gave the country a personal shape. Relatives became friends, and family history became something she could experience rather than simply hear about. Her affection for the culture grew through those connections.

The move was not permanent. Julia says her mother decided after about a year that they should return to Australia. That early reversal matters because her story does not begin with one decisive homecoming that settled everything. Even the first attempt involved uncertainty about where the family could make a life.

It also left something behind that did not disappear when she boarded the return flight: people she wanted to see again. The practical experiment had ended, but the relationships continued to influence her choices. A country that had initially felt unfamiliar now contained cousins whose lives mattered to her.

The distinction between residence and connection runs through the rest of the interview. Julia did not need to live continuously in Croatia for the place to remain important. Visits, family ties and changing circumstances kept bringing it back into view.

## Choosing to come back as a young adult

Julia returned with her sister in 1989. She was nineteen and her sister was sixteen. She remembers taking out a bank loan to fund the trip and spending about three months visiting cousins, travelling and enjoying their company.

This time, returning was her own choice. The contrast with arriving as a confused teenager is striking: she now knew whom she wanted to see and why she wanted to be there. The country had become associated with relationships and experiences she actively sought out.

Her memories of those years remain affectionate. Later in the conversation she describes herself as an “80s girl” and talks about an atmosphere she still finds appealing. These are personal impressions, shaped by the period in which she first formed close connections with Croatia. They should be understood as part of her attachment rather than as a claim that the country has remained frozen in time.

A long gap between visits followed. The interview moves across decades rather than presenting a continuous relocation diary. That makes the later returns important on their own terms: she was not repeating exactly the same trip, and she was not the same person each time she arrived.

By the time she came back in 2006, the question was no longer only how to spend time with cousins. Croatia was becoming a place where she could seek family support and reconsider the direction of her own life.

## Family, a familiar house and a place of safety

Julia says her parents returned permanently in 2005, after her mother inherited the family house. The home provided a continuing connection between generations, and it is the house from which Julia speaks in the interview.

She returned in 2006 after a difficult period in Australia. In the conversation, she describes leaving a severely abusive relationship and seeking safety. This is her account of what brought her back; the article does not attempt to investigate that history or identify the other person involved.

What matters to the story she tells is the role Croatia played at that point. It was a place where family and a sense of home were available when she needed distance from her previous circumstances. The emotional significance of the return cannot be reduced to a search for cheaper living or an attractive destination.

Her account also makes clear that finding support did not mean every family relationship had always been simple. Later, she describes returning to care for her parents as a healing experience, while acknowledging that she had lived independently since she was young. Coming back involved renegotiating family life as an adult.

The house therefore represents more than a convenient address. It connects inheritance, childhood memories, care and the practical work of rebuilding. Those layers help explain why family remains her strongest reason for being in Croatia, even when other parts of the move are difficult.

## Citizenship records and the name she was born with

Julia dates her citizenship application to 2006. She describes beginning the search for records in her father’s hometown, then having to look in Zagreb, where she had been born. An important complication was that the name in her birth records was not the name she commonly used in Australia.

She says she was born as Vlatka, with Julia becoming her first name in Australia and Vlatka remaining as a middle name. Establishing the connection between those names was part of resolving the paperwork. In her recollection, finding the records and completing that stage took about four months.

That is a description of her own historical case, not a current processing estimate. The interview does not establish which legal provision applied, and being born in Zagreb gives her circumstances a different starting point from those of someone researching a distant emigrant ancestor. Her experience should not be converted into a universal citizenship-by-descent checklist.

The practical difficulty in Julia’s account is nevertheless easy to understand: a person’s identity can be obvious to family while the documentary link still needs to be established. Her story sits alongside [Carl’s citizenship journey](../welcome-to-the-community/) as another example of why names and records deserve careful attention, without suggesting that their applications followed the same route.

## A pandemic visit that changed the direction of her life

The next major turning point came in 2020. Julia was living alone in Australia and describes feeling deeply isolated. During that period, she heard from her father that her mother was unwell. The distance between them became more difficult to bear.

She travelled to Croatia at the end of September, initially expecting to return to Australia in January. What had been planned as a limited stay became much longer after repeated flight cancellations. Julia recalls that the visit eventually lasted around ten months.

During that extended time, she began asking whether she wanted to return to her previous life at all. She remembers walking in Zadar and realising that she did not want to leave. The interview captures that moment as a personal change in perspective, rather than proof that a long visit is enough to settle every relocation question.

She still felt that she needed to return to Australia to deal with the business, car and other parts of the life she had built there. The desire to move and the work of leaving were separate tasks. Her account of cancelled flights and a difficult return journey explains how emotionally charged that period became.

After returning to Australia, she came back to Croatia within about five months, according to her recollection. The sequence was messy and disruptive. It did not resemble an orderly plan in which every administrative, financial and family decision was completed before the move began.

## Missing Australia without abandoning Croatia

When asked what she misses, Julia talks first about community. She had run a business in Australia for many years and had built relationships around it. Leaving meant losing the easy familiarity of people who already knew her and the routines through which those relationships were maintained.

She also misses her siblings, particularly her sisters, and the ease of speaking English. Although she can communicate in Croatian, she describes the language as demanding. Being able to speak does not always mean being able to express something as effortlessly or precisely as she could in English.

These observations complicate a simple before-and-after story. Croatia can be a place of peace while Australia remains the location of important relationships and a familiar working identity. Missing those things does not cancel the reasons for moving. It shows what the move cost alongside what it offered.

Julia also recognises that unfamiliar systems might have been difficult in another destination. Her comments do not present every obstacle as uniquely Croatian. They reflect the effort of moving from a country where she understood everyday arrangements to one where she had to learn them.

For readers considering a similar change, that distinction is useful. The interview invites attention to the specific things that make life feel easy: language, trusted contacts, known procedures and a routine. Those are forms of familiarity that can take time to rebuild even when a person has strong family roots in the destination.

## Registration and the effort of becoming established

Citizenship did not remove every administrative difficulty. Julia describes problems establishing her residence after returning, including repeated visits to verify that she actually lived at the address. She remembers four inspections before the matter was resolved.

The interview uses everyday language for this process, and the account is not detailed enough to identify every administrative distinction involved. The important point is her experience of having to demonstrate something that felt self-evident to her: she had come back to live there.

It would be misleading to present the number of visits as a standard requirement. It is a detail from Julia’s case. For someone planning a move, the relevant next step is to confirm the current address-registration requirements for their own status, rather than expecting to reproduce what happened to her.

The episode adds another layer to her advice about nostalgia. Feeling at home and becoming established in official systems can be separate experiences. Family connections may provide emotional support, but they do not necessarily replace the need to understand the procedure in front of you.

## Rebuilding a working identity when the first plan changes

Julia initially expected to return to work she already understood. Her background included accommodation, and she discussed the possibility of opening a rental agency. That plan did not develop as she had hoped.

She says she chose not to invest when she was not confident about the arrangement. The article does not treat that concern as evidence of wrongdoing by anyone else. It is part of her explanation of why she stepped back from a proposed business direction.

The disappointment was about more than income. Julia describes how not working left her feeling that she had lost an important part of her identity. In Australia, work and business ownership had helped define her routines and her place in a community. Starting again meant finding another way to use her abilities.

She taught English online to children in different countries and undertook interior-design projects through friends, including work in Pula. These were ways of working while the original idea changed, rather than evidence that she had arrived with one complete career plan.

Her current direction is dog grooming. The shift may sound unexpected beside accommodation and design, but in the interview it is presented as a practical next chapter. The work available to her, the skills she was developing and the life she was now living had moved beyond the picture she brought from Australia.

## A wine cellar, a new skill and a deliberately slower business plan

Beneath the family house is an old wine cellar where Julia remembers crushing grapes with her grandfather. She has considered turning that space into a dog-grooming business, linking a possible future livelihood with a place full of family memories.

At the time of the interview, however, she had decided to gain more experience first. Her plan was to work for the company that trained her before committing to her own operation. She discussed possibly investing after about a year, while making clear that this was a plan rather than an already-open business.

That choice is one of the most concrete examples of adaptation in the conversation. Instead of treating business ownership as something she had to recreate immediately, she was allowing time to become comfortable with the new work. The sequence had changed from her original expectations.

It also keeps the story open. The interview is not an advertisement for a salon or a claim that the cellar conversion has been approved or completed. It records what Julia was considering when she spoke with Carl. Any later opening would be a separate development to report in its own right.

## Peace, higher costs and a more complicated idea of a good life

When Julia describes what she has found in Croatia, she returns to a sense of peace and greater comfort with herself. She contrasts this with the stress she remembers from her life in Australia. Those feelings are central to her account, even though they cannot be measured through the practical details of a move.

At the same time, she says living costs have challenged her earlier expectation that Australian funds would stretch comfortably. She associates a change in affordability with the transition from kuna to euro. That is her experience of her budget; the interview does not establish how much national prices changed or what caused any particular increase.

**Editorial context:** Croatia adopted the euro on 1 January 2023. The currency changeover is a specific event, and should not be confused with joining the European Union or used on its own to explain household inflation. [European Central Bank: Croatia’s euro changeover](https://www.ecb.europa.eu/euro/changeover/croatia/html/index.en.html).

Julia also values the opportunities to travel from her Croatian base. She enjoys being able to consider other European destinations and describes that access as a benefit of her location. Her enthusiasm belongs beside the financial caution, rather than replacing it. She can appreciate the possibilities while revising what she assumed everyday life would cost.

## Family remains the reason, even when the future is uncertain

By the end of the conversation, Julia has moved away from the idea that she must know exactly where she will be in five years. She had expected to live in Zadar and work in hospitality. Instead, she renovated her parents’ home, created an apartment upstairs and remained closely involved in family life.

Her mother’s death changed that picture again. Julia says her father now relies on her, and she acknowledges feeling constrained by that responsibility while also recognising its importance. The interview allows both feelings to exist without forcing them into a simple account of either sacrifice or fulfilment.

That honesty is what makes her advice about removing nostalgia persuasive. Her connection to Croatia is deep, but it does not make the future predictable. Work, grief, care and personal preference continue to shape what returning means.

For anyone with Croatian roots, Julia’s story offers a fuller question than whether moving back is a good idea in the abstract. It asks what brings you there, which relationships matter, what practical life you can build and how willing you are to revise the first plan. Her answer is personal and unfinished: Croatia has become a place of family and peace, while starting again remains something she is actively doing.
