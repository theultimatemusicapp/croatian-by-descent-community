_Adapted from Anamaria’s interview with Carl Tomich, using the published video description and transcript. Her experiences, opinions and timings are presented as her own account. Citizenship, residence and customs rules depend on individual circumstances and can change, so readers should confirm current requirements with the responsible Croatian authority._

When Anamaria left California for Croatia with her young daughter, she was not testing a temporary escape or chasing a holiday version of the Adriatic. She was returning to the part of Croatia from which her family came, bringing her parents’ belongings, her own memories and a belief that life in Promina could offer something the constant pace of San Diego no longer did.

The move was physically and emotionally substantial. Her family packed more than four decades of possessions into a 40-foot shipping container. She gathered American certificates and court documents, arranged translations, established residence and applied for Croatian citizenship after arriving. The container took more than 90 days to reach Croatia, then spent additional time at the port before it could be transported and lifted into position by crane.

Yet the most important part of her story is not the container or a single administrative process. It is the contrast between the life she left and the one she is building in Marasovine, in the Promina area of Šibenik-Knin County. In California, she remembers relentless responsibility and little time to stop. In Croatia, she describes peace, family nearby, birds in the morning and the sound of bells on cattle passing through the countryside.

Her return has also led to new work. Alongside a role in marketing at a nearby immersive museum, Anamaria and a friend have started [Pure Promina](https://www.instagram.com/purepromina/), a community project created to showcase the people, small businesses, events and everyday life of the Promina region. The idea connects directly to her reason for returning: not simply to live in Croatia, but to take part in the place her family taught her to call home.

## From Cleveland and San Diego to her family’s homeland

Anamaria was born in Cleveland, Ohio, and spent most of her life in San Diego, California. Both of her parents were born in Croatia. Her father comes from the Promina area, while her mother was born in Zagreb into a large family whose earlier children were born in the Knin area. Her parents built their lives in the United States, but their Croatian roots remained central to family life.

That connection shaped how Anamaria understood herself. In the interview, she says she always felt proud to be Croatian and repeatedly felt drawn back after visits. “My heart has always been here,” she says. The sentence captures the emotional foundation of the move, but it did not remove the need to make difficult practical decisions.

Her parents were planning their own return in retirement. Anamaria had also gone through a divorce and had to determine what kind of future would be best for her and her daughter. She describes the decision to move as a strong instinct that they would thrive in Croatia. The interview makes clear that this was not a casual relocation. It followed legal and family arrangements specific to her circumstances, including court documents allowing her daughter to move.

Anamaria had prioritised the Croatian language from the beginning of her daughter’s life. Her daughter heard and used Croatian while learning to speak, because Anamaria believed English would remain widely available but Croatian might be much harder to acquire later. That choice became especially meaningful once they moved. Her daughter could communicate with relatives and other children, and the transition into local life and school did not begin with the same language barrier many returning families face.

Language is only one part of belonging, but in this family it created immediate connections. A child could walk to relatives and neighbours, take part in ordinary conversations and experience the community as a lived environment rather than as an inherited story. For Anamaria, that helped turn the idea of returning into something her daughter could also make her own.

## Moving an entire household across an ocean

International relocation often gets reduced to flights, documents and a final arrival photograph. Anamaria’s account shows the less glamorous work behind it. Her family bought a certified 40-foot shipping container and filled it with possessions collected over roughly 40 years in the United States.

The first decision was whether the contents justified the expense and risk. A container suitable for international freight is not the same as an ordinary storage container placed on private land. There were shipping specifications, a list of prohibited items and the broader question of what was worth carrying across the Atlantic.

Not every decision was financial. Some objects mattered because of family history rather than replacement cost. Anamaria describes asking herself how she would feel if the ship went down and the contents were lost. Moving required her to decide which objects were important enough to send and then accept that anything placed in the container was temporarily outside her control.

The estimated journey was shorter than the reality. She says they were told that shipping might take around 30 to 60 days depending on the season. Their container took more than 90 days to travel from California to Croatia. It then waited at the port for about another week while inspections and collection arrangements were handled.

Her most useful packing advice follows directly from that delay: do not put anything in the container that you will need immediately. Essential clothes, documents, medication, work equipment and irreplaceable daily items should travel with the person when possible, not sit inside freight whose arrival date can move. The exact packing list will differ for every household, but the principle is widely useful: plan for the shipment to arrive later than the optimistic estimate.

Final delivery involved coordination on the ground. A truck transported the container from the port and a crane was needed to lift it into position. Those details mattered because the shipment did not end when the vessel reached Croatia. Access, timing, unloading and placement were separate parts of the move.

Anamaria’s family wanted the move to be complete. Apart from close relatives who remained in the United States, she describes them as having cut their practical ties rather than leaving an entire household in storage as a fallback. The container therefore represented more than belongings. It was evidence of a family relocating its centre of life.

## What her customs experience can—and cannot—tell another returnee

In the interview, Anamaria explains that her father obtained a letter from the Croatian consulate confirming his move. The family also supplied proof that he had been living in San Diego, such as insurance or phone records. In her account, this documentation supported the treatment of their household possessions when the container entered Croatia.

That experience should not be turned into a universal promise that one letter automatically removes every tax or customs charge. The official rules contain eligibility conditions, evidence requirements and limits. Croatia is part of the European Union customs territory, and different rules may apply depending on where a person previously lived, how long they lived there, whether the goods were owned and used before the move, what is being imported and when it arrives.

The Croatian Customs Administration says relief may be available for personal property brought by someone transferring their usual residence from a non-EU country, subject to conditions. Its guidance discusses prior residence outside the Union, previous ownership and use of household goods, and an application to the relevant customs office. Readers planning a move should use [the official guidance for returnees and immigrants](https://carina.gov.hr/print.aspx?id=6708&url=print) as a starting point and obtain advice for their own shipment before it leaves.

Anamaria’s practical lesson remains valuable even where the paperwork differs: start early and collect evidence of the life you are leaving. Records that show residence, ownership and use can be far easier to obtain while accounts are active and documents are close at hand. Ask the carrier or customs representative for a written list of required documents and restricted items, and keep important originals outside the container.

## Applying for Croatian citizenship after arriving

Before moving, Anamaria had begun discussing her citizenship application with the Croatian consulate in Los Angeles. She provided documents and learned about the expected process if she continued from the United States. Because she was relocating, she says the consulate suggested that she take her records to Croatia and deal with the competent authorities after establishing residence.

Her document file was extensive. She mentions birth, marriage and divorce certificates, court and child-custody records, FBI-related documentation and fingerprints. Foreign public documents needed to be prepared for use in Croatia, and she paid for translation by a certified court interpreter who could handle the legal material. She describes translation and document preparation as expensive.

After arrival, residence came first in her sequence. She had a Croatian address and evidence that the family had moved. She says her residence was approved in about two weeks, initially for a year because the official expected the citizenship matter to be resolved within that period.

The citizenship decision took longer than the first informal estimate. Anamaria remembers being told it might take a month or two, then receiving the result after roughly four months. That is her personal timeline, not a current service standard. The legal basis, applicant history, office, record availability and need for further checks can all affect a case.

She also recalls a police visit to confirm her circumstances. Coming from the United States, she initially found the idea of police arriving at the house intimidating. In her telling, the visit was straightforward: the officer wanted to understand whether she lived there and why she had come.

Anamaria believes her case was easier because both parents were born in Croatia and she could provide evidence connecting them to Croatian citizenship. Other applicants may be dealing with a grandparent or more distant ancestor, missing records, name changes or a different legal category. Her story therefore demonstrates the value of organised evidence, but it does not establish that every child of Croatian emigrants will follow the same steps.

The Ministry of the Interior distinguishes between procedures for acquiring and determining citizenship and identifies different filing locations depending on whether a person has approved residence in Croatia. It also describes document legalisation, translations and category-specific evidence. Anyone considering an application should read the [Ministry’s current citizenship guidance](https://mup.gov.hr/aliens-281621/citizenship/281629) and confirm which legal route applies before relying on another applicant’s checklist.

## Leaving a relentless California chapter behind

Anamaria’s reasons for moving become clearer when she describes her final years in the United States. She had long wanted to own a business and began developing a café project in 2018. After a lengthy approval and construction process, the café opened in June 2020, during the COVID-19 pandemic.

Croatian identity appeared in the business as well as at home. The café served palačinke and family-made Croatian cakes and biscuits, including čupavci and walnut cookies. Croatian and American flags hung inside. Anamaria says she could not imagine building the place without including the culture that was such a large part of who she was.

The timing made an already demanding undertaking harder. She describes conflict over pandemic restrictions and a determination to keep the café operating for its customers. Those comments reflect her personal view of that period. The interview is not an investigation of the café’s compliance history, and her recollection should not be treated as public-health advice.

What matters for her relocation story is the pressure she remembers. Costs rose, the business carried a large financial burden and she was managing staff while pregnant and later caring for a baby. She also describes an unhappy marriage and says responsibility seemed to sit constantly on her shoulders. Even with family and friends nearby, daily life had become a continuous cycle of work and strain.

The move to Croatia did not erase the difficulty of those years. It changed the conditions around her. She no longer describes every day as “go, go, go.” Instead, she talks about being able to notice ordinary things: birds outside, cattle bells, neighbours and the feeling that she has time to inhabit the place where she lives.

## A slower life measured through family and safety

When asked how her life changed after the move, Anamaria calls it a complete reversal. “I have peace. I have happiness. I have family,” she says. These are subjective measures, but they are the measures that matter most in her account.

Her daughter’s experience is central. Anamaria describes her walking over to visit relatives and neighbours with the confidence of a child who knows the people around her. She believes that if her daughter is hungry, thirsty or needs help, someone nearby will look after her. That network gives Anamaria a sense of safety she did not feel in the same way in California.

The advantages she sees are not framed as an American arriving to improve Croatia. In fact, she resists that idea. She recognises that years in the United States gave her a strong preference for structure and immediate action, while everyday life in Croatia often feels less hurried. Yet she says she came to live a Croatian life rather than impose an American one.

That does not mean she idealises every difference. Her description of bureaucracy includes waiting and uncertainty. The container was expensive and late. Translation cost money. Returning required patience as well as affection for the country. Her happiness is more credible because she does not pretend the move was effortless.

She also sees Croatia’s scale as an advantage. From Promina, she can reach the coast for a day at the beach, return inland and spend time hiking around Promina or Dinara. The country can feel geographically compact while offering a surprising range of landscapes and experiences.

For Anamaria, however, the deepest benefit is social rather than scenic. Fruit and vegetables come from gardens. Family and friends are close. Support can arrive with unsolicited opinions attached, but it arrives. That combination of proximity, responsibility and shared life is what she believes makes Croatia a particularly good place to raise a family.

## New work connected to returnee stories

Anamaria did not arrive with every part of her new working life arranged. Opportunities developed after the move. She began working in marketing for Etnoland, an immersive museum roughly a 35-minute drive from her home, where she films content and supports social media.

The role carries a personal connection because the attraction was also created by a returnee. Anamaria describes the pleasure of working in a place that tells local stories, built by someone who came back to Croatia, while she is herself establishing a life after return. The work lets her use business and content skills developed in the United States without trying to recreate her California life exactly.

That distinction matters. Returning does not always mean abandoning previous experience, and it does not require importing an old career unchanged. Skills can be redirected toward local organisations, different audiences and a new pace of work. Anamaria’s marketing role is one example of that adaptation.

## Pure Promina: building visibility for Croatia’s inland communities

Her second project is even more closely tied to where she now lives. Anamaria and a friend are developing Pure Promina to promote the Promina region and strengthen the community around it.

Promina lies inland in Šibenik-Knin County, within reach of the coast but outside the standard island-and-beach picture used to market Croatia. Anamaria believes visitors can focus so heavily on the Adriatic that they miss the landscape, villages, businesses and slower rhythm of the hinterland.

Pure Promina is their answer to that gap. In the interview, she says they want to create events, promote local family farms and businesses, uplift the wider community and show the value of the Croatian way of life. The project is both outward-facing and local: it can introduce visitors and members of the diaspora to the region while giving people who already live there another platform for their work.

That mission grows naturally from Anamaria’s story. She returned because of family roots in Promina, then discovered professional possibilities rooted in the same place. Rather than treating the region as a quiet backdrop to her personal reinvention, she and her friend are trying to make its people and activity more visible.

Readers can follow the project on Instagram at [@purepromina](https://www.instagram.com/purepromina/). At the time of this interview, Anamaria describes Pure Promina as something they are building, not as a finished tourism authority or a promise that every planned event has already launched. Following the account is the best way to see what they publish next and to contact the project directly about local collaborations.

## What prospective returnees can take from Anamaria’s experience

Anamaria’s advice is emotionally simple: if the move is right for you, come. The practical story underneath that advice is more demanding. She gathered official records, resolved family arrangements, prepared a transatlantic shipment, paid for translations, established an address and allowed time for processes that did not always meet the first estimate.

Her experience suggests several useful questions for anyone planning a similar return:

- Which legal process applies to your citizenship or residence circumstances, and which authority can confirm it?
- Can you obtain fresh civil, police and court records before leaving your current country?
- Do foreign documents require an apostille, legalisation or certified Croatian translation?
- Is shipping a full household financially and emotionally worthwhile, and which items must travel with you instead?
- What evidence will customs ask for to show your previous residence and your ownership and use of household goods?
- Where will you live on arrival, and what must be in place before you can register that address?
- How will children manage language, school, family contact and the change in routine?
- What work can you begin immediately, and which longer-term business ideas need time to develop?

The answers will not be identical to Anamaria’s. Her parents’ birth in Croatia, her daughter’s Croatian language, her family property and her move from the United States all shaped the path she followed. Another returnee may have different citizenship eligibility, customs treatment, finances or support networks.

What can be shared is the value of preparation combined with flexibility. The container arrived late, the citizenship decision took longer than first suggested and her work appeared in forms she had not planned in California. The move succeeded for her not because every detail followed a perfect schedule, but because the reason for returning was strong enough to survive changing logistics.

## A return that became a new beginning

Anamaria says that after she arrived, the pieces of her new life began to appear: work, a business project, community and a safer rhythm for her daughter. “It is the best decision that I ever made,” she says.

That conclusion belongs to her. A move to Croatia will not solve every person’s difficulties, and rural life will not fit everyone. Yet her interview gives Croatian Americans and other members of the diaspora something more useful than a universal verdict. It shows one family making a serious return, dealing with the unglamorous work and finding that the emotional idea of home could become an everyday reality.

Promina is no longer only where her father came from or where she spent visits. It is the place where her daughter walks to family, where Anamaria works, and where Pure Promina is beginning to tell local stories. The journey from California ended with more than an arrival. It opened a chapter in which ancestry, parenthood and community building now occupy the same landscape.
